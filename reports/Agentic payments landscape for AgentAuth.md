# Agent payment standards leave corporate authority unclaimed

As of 21 October 2026, every published agentic payment protocol (Google AP2, Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol, Mastercard Agent Pay and Verifiable Intent, OpenAI/Stripe ACP, x402, and the EMVCo draft) models **a consumer or an agent paying a merchant or a resource**. None of them specifies how a company delegates authority to its own agents to issue refunds, payouts or vendor payments, routes exceptions to two approvers, keeps duties separate, or proves afterwards what was authorized. Regulators in the EU, US, UK, Singapore and India have not written agent-specific payment rules either. Where liability is discussed, it turns on one fact, the **scope of authority the principal granted**, and that is exactly the record AgentAuth produces. Underneath, the payment APIs that agents call are asynchronous and reversible. A Stripe refund marked `succeeded` can turn `failed` up to about 30 days later, and a `paid` payout can turn `failed`. A budget that counts money as spent when the API first returns a response will be wrong, and an agent that retries with a fresh idempotency key after a timeout can pay twice. The opening for AgentAuth is a rail-neutral authority, approval and evidence layer for internal finance agents. It should accept mandates in the AP2 / Verifiable Intent credential format but not adopt their consumer data model, it should keep a pending-aware budget ledger, and its claims should stay strictly bounded. No court or regulator has yet said that signed mandate records establish authorization.

**How to read this report.** Statements cited to API documentation, standards text, regulator publications or SEC filings are treated as **verified facts**. Company announcements, blogs and earnings-call figures are marked as **vendor claims**. Law-firm commentary is marked as **interpretation**. Statements headed "for AgentAuth" are this report's own **inferences**. Some EU and US statutory provisions were restated from well-known text and not re-fetched during the research; they are flagged where they appear.

## Payment APIs report graded, reversible states, never a single "done"

Provider APIs separate *request accepted*, *money in motion* and *money settled*, and each of those can still be reversed. Stripe's PaymentIntent has seven statuses and **no terminal "failed" state**. A decline sends the intent back to `requires_payment_method` so it can be retried. `processing` can last "up to a few days" for bank debits, and only `succeeded` means "the funds are now in your account" ([Stripe lifecycle](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/paymentintents/lifecycle)). After that, refunds and disputes appear on the Charge while the PaymentIntent stays `succeeded` ([Stripe status updates](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/payment-intents/verifying-status)). Refunds have their own state machine (`pending`, `requires_action`, `succeeded`, `failed`, `canceled`). A refund can fail after it was initiated, with the bank returning funds **"up to 30 days from the post date"**, and if the balance is too low, non-card refunds fail outright ([Stripe refunds](https://docs.stripe.com/refunds)). Payouts move through `pending` → `in_transit` → `paid`, but "some payouts that fail might initially show as `paid`, then change to `failed`" ([Stripe Payout object](https://docs.stripe.com/api/payouts/object)). Adyen is asynchronous by design. Every capture, refund, cancel or reversal returns `status: "received"`, and the outcome arrives in a webhook. Even `success: true` only means Adyen's validations passed: `CAPTURE_FAILED` can arrive "a few days" later, refunds can be followed by `REFUND_FAILED` or `REFUNDED_REVERSED`, and funds can take "up to 40 business days" to reach the shopper ([Adyen capture](https://docs.adyen.com/online-payments/capture); [Adyen refund](https://docs.adyen.com/online-payments/refund)). ISO 20022 works the same way: pacs.002 and pain.002 report graded codes (RCVD, ACTC, ACCP, ACSP, ACSC, plus PDNG for holds and RJCT with a mandatory reason), and banks implement different subsets of them (secondary, [Mambu Payments](https://docs.mambupayments.com/docs/pacs002-xsd-business-logic)).

Card authorizations reserve money; they do not move it. An authorization places a hold that "is meant to be settled", and Visa requires the full amount to be reversed **within 24 hours** if the sale will not complete, or the difference reversed if the final amount is lower ([Visa authorization and reversal requirements](https://usa.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/regional/na/us/support-legal/documents/authorization-and-reversal-processing-best-practices-for-merchants.pdf)). Under Stripe manual capture, an intent waits in `requires_capture` and, if not captured in time, is `canceled` and the funds released. Card-not-present validity is about 5 days for merchant-initiated Visa transactions and 7 days for most others. A partial capture releases the remainder and normally rules out any further capture, and an uncaptured intent cannot be refunded, only cancelled ([Stripe place a hold](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method); [Stripe refunds](https://docs.stripe.com/refunds)).

Idempotency and webhook semantics are well defined and differ by provider in ways a gateway has to absorb. The table below compares the two providers researched.

| Behaviour | Stripe (API v1) | Adyen |
|---|---|---|
| Key header and length | `Idempotency-Key`, ≤255 chars | `idempotency-key`, ≤64 chars |
| Retention | Pruned after ≥24 h; API v2 uses a 30-day window | 7–14 days, company-account scope, not checked across regions |
| Replay | Saved status and body, **including 500s**; `Idempotent-Replayed: true` | First response "without duplication" |
| Concurrent duplicate | 409 conflict; retry with the same key | 422/409, code 704 |
| Payload mismatch | Error | Not documented |
| Webhooks | At-least-once, unordered, retried up to 3 days; dedupe by event ID | 2xx within 10 s; dedupe by `eventCode` + `pspReference` |

Sources: [Stripe idempotent requests](https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests), [Stripe advanced error handling](https://docs.stripe.com/error-low-level), [Stripe webhooks](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks), [Adyen idempotency](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/api-idempotency), [Adyen webhooks](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/webhooks/handle-webhook-events).

Stripe's retry guidance is the rule that matters most for an agent gateway. A 500 is indeterminate ("the operation might have produced side effects"), and the guidance says: **"Don't retry with a new idempotency key until you've resolved the original outcome."** Keep retrying with the same key for up to 24 hours, then reconcile through retrieval and webhooks ([Stripe error handling](https://docs.stripe.com/error-low-level)). The IETF Idempotency-Key header draft sets out the generic contract (409 while a request is in flight, 422 when a key is reused with a different payload, a published expiry policy), but it **expired and is archived, so it is not an RFC** ([IETF datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-httpapi-idempotency-key-header/)).

## Protocols specify consumer mandates, not corporate authority or liability

The protocols are converging on a common credential vocabulary, which is useful to AgentAuth, but they model the wrong principal and the wrong flows. The table below summarizes what each one actually specifies.

AP2 is the most fully specified, and the details are verified from its site. A Closed mandate is signed by the user, who is present. An Open mandate is signed by the user ahead of time and later closed by the agent, which proves possession of the key named in the mandate's `cnf` claim (RFC 7800). Verifiers "MUST return a signed Mandate Receipt", and credentials are presented through OpenID4VP ([AP2 Agent Authorization](https://ap2-protocol.org/ap2/agent_authorization/); [ap2-protocol.org](https://ap2-protocol.org/)). AP2's own repository calls itself "code samples and demos", with installation from Git and a PyPI package "planned" ([GitHub AP2](https://github.com/google-agentic-commerce/AP2)). The v0.1 terms Intent/Cart/Payment Mandate appear to have been renamed to Checkout/Payment with Open/Closed forms in v0.2. That is this report's reading of the site; the full v0.2 specification returned a 404 during the research. FIDO now hosts an Agentic Authentication working group and a Payments working group chaired by Mastercard and Visa members, and their first contributions are AP2 and Verifiable Intent ([FIDO](https://fidoalliance.org/google-donates-agent-payments-protocol-to-fido-alliance/); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/fido-alliance-forms-working-groups-to-lock-down-ai-agent-payments/)). It is reported, but unverified, that Verifiable Intent uses an SD-JWT delegation chain with eight constraint types mapped to AP2, UCP and ACP ([stellagent, secondary](https://stellagent.ai/insights/mastercard-verifiable-intent)). If accurate, SD-JWT VC with key binding is becoming the shared mandate format.

The B2B signals are product pilots, not standards. Visa's 20 October 2026 release describes the "first live pilot" in which a BILL agent pays supplier invoices through Billtrust. The agent "only executes eligible payments that have already been approved by the buyer", and that approval happens inside BILL ([Visa press release](https://usa.visa.com/about-visa/newsroom/press-releases.releaseId.22856.html)). Stripe's MCP server is closest to AgentAuth's use case, since it is how an agent issues refunds and sends Treasury `send_money` / `transfer_money` from the company's own account. Its control is a single-user, per-action confirmation link that expires after 24 hours, together with a recommendation to enable the MCP client's tool approval ([Stripe MCP](https://docs.stripe.com/mcp)). It has no thresholds, no approver roles and no exportable evidence. Some market facts rest on weaker sourcing and should not be repeated as fact. These include Mastercard's registration and liability rules (its pages could not be fetched), the reported scaling-back of ChatGPT Instant Checkout in March 2026 ([Storyboard18](https://storyboard18.com/digital/openai-reduces-chatgpt-shopping-focus-shifts-to-product-discovery-93192.html)), a Ramp–Visa corporate bill-pay partnership, and claims of 30 or more European issuers live on VIC.

No jurisdiction reviewed has an agent-specific payment rule in force. The table below gives the position as of 21 October 2026.

Read together, these sources point to three conclusions. First, **dynamic linking makes open-ended "spend as needed" grants incompatible with per-payment SCA in the EU**. The defensible pattern is SCA when the mandate is set up, followed by payments inside a pre-authorized scope that fits an exemption (merchant-initiated or recurring, trusted beneficiary, low value, or the Art. 17 corporate exemption if the competent authority accepts it). This is an inference from the statutory text; there is no EBA opinion on agents. Second, the vocabulary is converging even though nothing binding has been written. Osborne Clarke recommends recording who consented, what the mandate covered, the limits, whether step-up was triggered, and what the agent did ([Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/agentic-payments-new-challenge-europes-payments-ecosystem)). Skadden expects HMT to emphasize mandates, parameters, audit trails and revocation. MAS SAFR describes pre-execution verification. Bank of England Deputy Governor Breeden said that "relying on a human in the loop for all agent actions is unlikely to be realistic" ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/what-new-rules-agentic-ai-payments-look-like)), which supports mandate-scoped autonomy with exception-based approval rather than approving every action. Third, AgentAuth's actual customers sit mostly outside consumer protection law. A company refunding its customers or paying vendors from its own business accounts is making commercial transfers under UCC 4A and its bank agreement (interpretation), and its auditors judge it against ITGC and segregation-of-duties expectations. Its regulatory exposure therefore runs mainly through SOX-style controls evidence and through its PSPs' demands for mandate evidence, not through Reg E. Fenwick's caveat applies to all of this: signed mandates "still untested in courts and before regulators" ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/is-2026-the-year-of-agentic-payments)).

## Seven controls no standard covers for a company's own agents

Human disbursement control rests on approver limits per person (the delegation of authority, or DoA), dual control on payment release and on vendor bank-detail changes, and logs that auditors test. Each of these lives inside one system's configuration. NetSuite approval rules use amount limits and approver chains ([Oracle NetSuite](https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/netsuite/ns-online-help/bridgehead_1544019693.html)), SAP dual control requires a second user to confirm sensitive vendor fields ([SAPinsider](https://sapinsider.org/monitor-sensitive-fields-with-r-3s-dual-control-functionality/)), and AP guidance says that whoever can change a vendor record should not release payment to that vendor ([Ramp](https://ramp.com/blog/accounts-payable/segregation-of-duties-in-accounts-payable)). Identity standards now cover the agent side well. Entra Agent ID gives each agent an accountable sponsor and expiring access ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/id-governance/agent-id-governance-overview)). Auth0 offers asynchronous CIBA approval with RAR transaction details ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/ai/docs/intro/asynchronous-authorization)). MCP authorization specifies OAuth 2.1 with resource indicators, forbids token passthrough, and defines no amount limits, approval workflows or audit logging ([MCP spec](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/basic/authorization)). The nearest standards draft, draft-liu-ai-agent-authorization-integration-00 (July 2026, an individual Informational draft with Okta among the authors), adds Rego per-request caps such as `amount <= 50` and AS-signed "authorization evidence", but **does not define cumulative budgets or running totals** ([IETF datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-liu-ai-agent-authorization-integration/)).

Comparing the human control model with these standards leaves seven uncovered controls. This list is an inference from what the sources do not contain, not a sourced claim that nobody offers them. The first is **per-agent spending authority as a DoA**: cumulative, per-period, per-counterparty limits that are capped at the sponsor's own authority. The second is **exception approval with dual control**: threshold routing, two distinct approvers, a check that the approver is not the initiator, and expiry. The third is **agent segregation of duties**: no single agent, and no two agents under one sponsor, can both edit vendor bank details and pay that vendor. The fourth is **auditor-verifiable evidence** linking mandate, policy decision, approval and execution. The fifth is **reversal linkage**: tying a refund, void or recall back to the original authorization. The sixth is **cross-system enforcement**, because Ramp, BILL and NetSuite limits each stop at their own boundary. The seventh is **non-card rails and corporate principals**, which the consumer protocols do not model.

Demand is real, but surveys measure intent rather than purchases. Deloitte's Q4 2025 CFO Signals found **54% of CFOs name integrating AI agents into finance as a top 2026 transformation priority** ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/business-strategy-growth/4q-2025-cfo-signals-survey.html)). KPMG's Q2 2026 pulse found **67% cite human-oversight skills as a top challenge** in deploying agents ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/pdf/2026/tech-aipulsesurvey-q2.pdf)). Agents have already moved from recommending to executing. BILL's FY2026 10-K reports its first suite of AI agents ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001786352/000162828026058238/bill-20260630.htm)), and BILL says, in an unverified third-party transcript, that a pay-for-you agent has completed "tens of thousands of card transactions without any human interaction" ([StatementDog](https://statementdog.com/analysis/BILL/earnings_calls/313379)). Yet a controller profiled by Ramp still releases payments in Ramp's own product, "where there is a full audit trail" ([Ramp](https://ramp.com/blog/ramp-customers-are-running-month-end-with-ai-assistants)). The evidence trail is what keeps humans in the loop at the release step, and it is the piece agents lack.

The competitive risk comes from two directions. Upstream, Stripe already requires human confirmation for refunds and outbound payments, and the move to Agent-tagged keys shows it intends to own agent credentials on its own platform. Each PSP or spend platform can ship "good enough" per-action approval within its own boundary. Sideways, the crowded agent-security group already sits in the MCP path with an Okta or Entra integration and can add amount thresholds. AgentAuth's defensible ground lies where neither group can reach: a single budget and approval state **across** Stripe, Adyen, bank APIs and ERPs, finance-grade controls (DoA ceilings, four-eyes approval, agent segregation of duties), and evidence a third party can verify without trusting AgentAuth's servers, which fits the self-hostable, no-cloud-dependency design in the project's own constraints.

## What this means for AgentAuth's design

**A budget ledger that follows the payment lifecycle.** Because no provider exposes a single "money moved" number, the ledger should be append-only per approved action and track five buckets. **Reserved** covers actions approved but not yet sent, plus authorization holds. **Committed** covers actions sent whose upstream status is still `processing`, `pending`, `in_transit` or Adyen `received`. **Settled** covers terminal successes, which can still reverse. **Released** covers cancels, expiries, uncaptured remainders, `failed` or `*_FAILED` outcomes and `REFUNDED_REVERSED`. **Unknown** covers 5xx responses and timeouts. Limits should be checked against reserved + committed + settled, so that money in flight cannot be spent twice, and a late `refund.failed` or payout `failed` should move an entry out of settled. Each entry should record the mandate and approval IDs, the agent identity, provider and endpoint, a hash of the request parameters, the gateway and upstream idempotency keys, the upstream object IDs (PaymentIntent, Refund, Payout, `pspReference`), amounts in minor units, and a state history keyed by provider event ID. The local ID should also be put into Stripe `metadata`, as Stripe recommends, so webhooks can be matched to records ([Stripe error handling](https://docs.stripe.com/error-low-level)). All of this is design inference, not something a source prescribes.

**Idempotency that refuses to guess.** For each approved authorization, mint exactly one upstream key and persist it before making the call. Return 409 while that call is in flight and 422 when a key is reused with a different payload, following the IETF draft. Retain keys for at least the longest upstream window, which means 14 days to cover Adyen rather than Stripe's 24 hours. Treat any indeterminate result as the **unknown** state, and block a re-issue until a retrieval or webhook resolves it. This is the gateway's most important safety property, because an agent that "helpfully" retries a timed-out payout with a new key causes the double payment Stripe warns about. Webhook ingestion should normalize Stripe event-ID deduplication and Adyen `eventCode` + `pspReference` deduplication into one event log, should not rely on arrival order, and should verify signatures (Stripe's HMAC with a tolerance that is never zero).

**Consent and step-up backed by SCA and passkeys.** The protocols and regulators agree on a pattern: strong authentication at mandate creation, then autonomy inside the mandate's scope, with step-up for anything outside it. AgentAuth should make mandate grants and exception approvals passkey-signed (WebAuthn), and the approval screen should show the amount and payee of each specific exception, following the logic of dynamic linking. That gives an approval which looks like SCA, and it matches HSBC/Mastercard's use of Payment Passkeys and AP2's mention of passkey-backed user keys. AgentAuth must not claim that its approvals satisfy PSD2 SCA. SCA is an obligation of the PSP, and under PSD3 delegated SCA is outsourcing for which the PSP remains liable. A defensible claim is that AgentAuth "records a phishing-resistant, transaction-specific human approval". Whether a bank accepts that as part of an Art. 17 corporate process is the bank's decision.

**Mandates in the AP2 / Verifiable Intent credential format, with a corporate data model.** AgentAuth should not adopt AP2's consumer Checkout/Payment schema as its internal model. It lacks approver chains, DoA ceilings, segregation-of-duties constraints, payout and refund action types, and non-card rails. It is also pre-1.0, and its terminology has already changed once. The better approach is to keep AgentAuth's own mandate schema, versioned according to the repository's storage-format rules, and add an **export profile** that emits an SD-JWT VC with a `cnf`-bound agent key and KB-JWT presentation. AgentAuth would then act as the "Trusted Agent Provider" or Credential Provider for open, pre-authorized mandates, so that a PSP or network can verify a mandate that originated inside the company. Once FIDO's Payments working group publishes, the mapping can follow it. Until then it should be labelled experimental, and it should never claim conformance to a specification that has no releases.

**Gateway enforcement that matches the actual rail.** Enforcement has to happen in the gateway, with AgentAuth holding the credentials (its broker), because MCP forbids token passthrough and Stripe's MCP accepts only scoped Agent keys from December 2026. The gateway should mint or hold Agent-tagged restricted keys and never hand agents full-access secrets. Customers should see Stripe's own confirmation as a second layer of defence, not something AgentAuth replaces. Coverage should be built rail by rail. Stripe refunds and payouts come first. Adyen modifications are needed for European merchants. Bank APIs and ISO 20022 status codes (ACSC as settled, RJCT with its reason as released) come next. Card holds can be modelled on Visa's 24-hour reversal rule. x402 or stablecoin payments come only if customers ask. On a rail where recall is weak or impossible (wires, RTP, payouts), the evidence should say so plainly instead of implying that the payment can be reversed.

**Positioning and claims.** The defensible pitch is *the authority, approval and evidence layer for agents that move a company's own money, across every rail it already uses*. That speaks to the 54% of CFOs prioritizing finance agents and the 67% who name oversight as the obstacle, and it maps to COSO's posting and orchestration capabilities without claiming COSO requires it. Evidence exports should state exactly what the verifier checks, for example "provider reported refund `succeeded` at time T; approval signed by key K over parameters H". They should not claim that funds arrived, that liability shifts, or that the record "proves authorization" in a dispute.

## Conclusion

The industry's identity and payment primitives, namely signed key-bound mandates, passkey approval, agent registration and tokenized credentials, are maturing quickly, but they are being built around one shape: a consumer delegating a purchase. AgentAuth's opportunity lies in the corporate version of the problem, where the questions differ: who may grant how much authority, who must co-approve, which duties must stay separate, and how an auditor checks it later across several rails. The two strongest moves are to treat the consumer protocols as **output formats** rather than data models, and to build the budget ledger around the uncomfortable fact that money in the payment APIs is provisional for days or weeks.

The main uncertainties look forward, not backward. The HM Treasury consultation outcome, the FIDO Payments working-group deliverables, the EMVCo framework after comments, and the MAS agentic consultation in 2027 could each turn today's "recommended practice" into a required record format, which would reward a vendor that already emits it. Platform risk from Stripe, Ramp and the agent-security group is real, but their incentives keep each of them within its own boundary. The cross-rail view is the one position none of them is set up to take.
