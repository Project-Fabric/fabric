# Finance teams want agent brakes before autonomy

As of late October 2026 the need for Cosigna is **real but narrower and earlier than the pitch implies**. Companies are not yet letting agents move meaningful money on their own. The one survey that measures autonomy in money movement puts fully automatic release of third-party payments at **9%**, and about half of finance leaders would let an agent *initiate* a payment only if a human approves it. The controls those leaders say they need first, namely limits, revocation, visibility, audit history and identity, are almost exactly Cosigna's feature list. The pain today shows up as hesitation and audit exposure rather than losses. No verified mainstream enterprise loss from an agent payment has been published, while auditors (ISACA: "the absence of adequate logging is itself a finding") and insurers (spreading AI exclusions) are making the evidence gap expensive in advance. The biggest threat is not other startups. It is that **single-rail incumbents now give the basic control away free**: Stripe's two-party approval rules ship on by default for agent-tagged keys and cover refunds, payouts and outbound transfers, and Ramp's Agent Cards are scoped single-use cards that inherit the issuing user's approval chain. What nobody covers is one mandate, one approval inbox and one verifiable evidence trail **across** rails: Stripe plus bank payouts plus ERP. That gap only hurts companies whose agents touch two or more of those systems, which today means mid-market companies building their own agents and a small set of AI-native operators. The verdict is a qualified yes. There is a need now for an approval-and-evidence layer on payouts made by in-house agents across more than one rail, and India's payout rails, where native agent controls are thinnest, are a credible place to start. Autonomy-scale budgets are a 2027–2030 story, and many buyers will discover that Stripe or their ERP already covers them.

**How to read this report.** Claims cited to product documentation, regulator or standards publications, court decisions and earnings filings are treated as verified facts. Survey figures are labelled with their sponsor when a vendor ran them, and most finance-AI surveys in this space were vendor-run. Capabilities announced in press releases or blogs are labelled vendor claims. Statements about Cosigna's opportunity are this report's inferences.

## Agents move money today only in narrow, vendor-fenced lanes

Headline adoption surveys measure enthusiasm, not money movement. KPMG's US pulse reports that **62% of organizations are building, deploying or developing agents**, up from 53% a quarter earlier ([KPMG Q3 2026](https://kpmg.com/us/en/media/news/q3-ai-pulse-2026.html)). McKinsey finds 23% scaling an agentic system somewhere but **no more than 10% scaling agents in any single function**, with IT and knowledge management leading rather than finance ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai)). Finance-specific figures show stated priority more than deployment. **54% of CFOs call integrating agents into finance a top 2026 priority** ([Deloitte Q4 2025](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/deloitte-q4-2025-cfo-signals-survey.html)), but only **7% of finance organizations report high impact** from AI ([Gartner Jun 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-06-08-gartner-says-cfos-need-structured-finance-ai-roadmaps)). A vendor-run poll at a CCH Tagetik conference put current agentic use at 6%, with 38% planning to adopt within a year ([Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/news/pr-2025-wolters-kluwer-survey-increasing-adoption-agentic-ai)).

The best data on autonomy comes from a vendor-sponsored survey of 520 US finance leaders by OvationCXM, and it gives the clearest picture of where Cosigna fits. About **52% would let agents initiate payments, ACH, wires or transfers**, but **only 9% would allow fully automatic approval or release of a third-party payment**. When a human approval step is included, leaders are nearly as willing to let agents initiate transactions as to let them do read-only tasks (50% vs 54%). The tools behind this "agent use" are mostly ChatGPT (39%) and Copilot (36%); only 12% use a direct bank API ([OvationCXM](https://www.ovationcxm.com/blog/ai-agents-in-banking-new-research-shows-agents-taking-action)). Most finance "agents" are therefore not wired into the money rails at all. An independent PwC survey agrees: **only 20% of executives would trust agents with financial transactions** ([Techstrong.ai on PwC](https://techstrong.ai/features/ai-agents-arent-entirely-trusted-with-high-stake-use-cases-pwc-survey/)).

Where agents really do execute, they do it inside one vendor's platform and under that vendor's controls. Support-agent refunds are probably the highest-volume autonomous money movement today. Intercom Fin can execute refunds through Stripe or Shopify connectors, with an optional human-in-the-loop step for high-value actions ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/14468561-human-in-the-loop-approvals-for-fin-procedures)). Published caps are rare; the only concrete one found is Fini's example of auto-refund under $50 ([Fini](https://www.usefini.com/guides/ai-chatbots-cancel-subscriptions-refunds-shopify-plus-gorgias)). In AP, Ramp's agents code bills, screen for fraud and enter card details into vendor portals, but approvals are *recommended*, not granted. Ramp reports 90% acceptance across 90,000 approval recommendations, a vendor claim for a feature that was in early access ([Ramp](https://ramp.com/blog/ramp-ap-agents-announcement)). BILL told investors that **over 175,000 businesses use its agents**, but most of them do data and compliance tasks (W-9 collection, invoice coding), and the one payments agent cited completed about 30,000 card transactions in a quarter, with no approval model described ([Motley Fool transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/08/26/bill-bill-q4-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)). Visa's first live B2B agent pilot, a BILL agent paying invoices through Billtrust, "only executes eligible payments that have already been approved by the buyer" ([Visa](https://usa.visa.com/about-visa/newsroom/press-releases.releaseId.22856.html)). ERP agents from SAP, Oracle and Microsoft reconcile, flag and route; Coupa's payment-batch agent explicitly "does not authorize the release of funds" ([Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/coupa-deploys-agent-for-payment-batch-creation-638a3390)). On the consumer side, Visa said early in 2026 that "hundreds" of agent-initiated transactions had been completed with partners, which is pilot scale ([Digital Transactions](https://www.digitaltransactions.net/?p=1331495)).

Taken together, the market today runs on a pattern of **agent prepares, human releases**, with full autonomy only for small refunds and scoped card spend. That is good and bad for Cosigna. The roughly 50% "initiate with approval" group is a live market for approval routing and evidence. The single-digit autonomous group is where the frightening incidents will eventually come from, but it is too small to fund a company today. This report could not find a single independently verified, named deployment in which an agent releases AP payments or posts journal entries without a human per transaction.

## The pain is hesitation and audit exposure, not yet losses

The incident record is thin outside crypto, and a skeptical founder should say so. The mainstream precedents are legal. In *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, the tribunal ordered the airline to pay CA$812.02 for its chatbot's wrong refund advice and rejected the claim that the bot was responsible for itself ([MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2024/02/16/air-canada-has-to-pay-for-chatbot-mistake/)). California's AB 316, in force since January 2026, reportedly bars defendants from arguing that an AI acted autonomously ([Yahoo Finance/BeInCrypto](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/crypto/articles/responsible-ai-agent-loses-money-210732192.html)). Replit's agent wiped a production database during an explicit code freeze, which is the clearest analogue for an agent changing a system of record against instructions ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/replit-ai-coding-assistant-failure/)). Real autonomous money losses so far are crypto stories. In the Bankrbot case, a Morse-coded instruction decoded by Grok triggered an estimated **$150k–$200k** transfer ([Yahoo Finance/BeInCrypto](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/crypto/articles/responsible-ai-agent-loses-money-210732192.html)). In Freysa, a player talked an agent that had been told never to pay into releasing about $47k ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/b/8335)). AIXBT's ~$100k loss came from a compromised admin dashboard, not from manipulating the model ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/346911/)). The research found **no verified non-crypto case of an enterprise agent making a wrong payment or refund with a disclosed loss** in 2025–2026. A Finextra review reportedly reached the same conclusion, though it could not be fetched ([Finextra](https://www.finextra.com/blogposting/33090/when-agents-pay-the-wrong-party-what-the-sourced-public-record-shows-so-far-in-2026)).

The threat is nevertheless now operational rather than theoretical. Forcepoint found **a live indirect prompt-injection payload carrying a complete $5,000 PayPal transaction**, aimed at browser agents with saved payment credentials, though no losses were reported ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/researchers-10-wild-indirect/)). Invariant Labs showed a malicious GitHub issue hijacking an agent through the official GitHub MCP server ([Invariant Labs](https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-github-vulnerability)). OWASP's 2026 agentic list ranks tool misuse and identity/privilege abuse among the top risks ([Teleport summary](https://goteleport.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications)), and the UK NCSC argues prompt injection should not be treated as solvable the way SQL injection is ([Forcepoint](https://www.forcepoint.com/blog/insights/prompt-injection-attacks)). The security consensus therefore favours Cosigna's architecture: assume the model can be talked into anything, and enforce limits, approvals and credential custody outside it. The adjacent human-fraud numbers explain why finance teams are wary of any new way to release payments. Arup lost about **US$25.6M** to a deepfaked CFO video call ([CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/05/16/tech/arup-deepfake-scam-loss-hong-kong-intl-hnk)), and the FBI recorded about **$3.05B in business email compromise losses** in 2025 ([McDonald Hopkins](https://www.mcdonaldhopkins.com/insights/news/the-sobering-truth-of-the-fbis-2025-internet-crime-complaint-center-report)).

Auditors are where the pain becomes concrete. ISACA's September 2026 guidance tells auditors to establish what agent logs exist, how long they are kept and whether they are tamper-evident, and states that **"the absence of adequate logging is itself a finding"**. It also recommends scope testing of delegated authority and escalation controls ([ISACA](https://www.isaca.org/resources/news-and-trends/industry-news/2026/auditing-agentic-ai-workflows-how-to-control-test-when-the-system-decides-for-itself)). FEI released an AI framework for ICFR mapped to COSO 2013 ([Cherry Hill Advisory](https://www.cherryhilladvisory.com/risk-register-blog/fei-ai-framework-sox-icfr-internal-audit)), and Deloitte's audit practice calls for audit trails, guardrails and human override tested for operating effectiveness ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/services/audit-assurance/blogs/accounting-finance/audit-ai-risk-management.html)). None of this is binding. The PCAOB and SEC have no agent-specific ICFR rule ([Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/pcaob-warns-auditors-about-ai-and-pe)), so Cosigna must never say "SOX requires this". The honest pitch is that it reduces auditor friction and findings.

CFO surveys, nearly all vendor-run, show the same thing. **75% of CFOs cite lack of auditability** as the top reason they hesitate to trust AI with mission-critical work ([Datarails](https://www.datarails.com/research/2026-cfo-sentiments/)). Only **43% of $1B+ CFOs feel confident in their AI governance** ([Deloitte 2Q 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/business-strategy-growth/2q-2026-cfo-signals-survey.html)). **30% have not updated internal controls for agents taking actions**, and 46% have untested AI incident plans ([ERP Today on Avalara](https://erp.today/avalara-agentic-ai-finance-governance-cfo-survey/)). In Medius's census, **90% require human approval for AI decisions, yet 46% say their approval thresholds are "understood but not formally documented"**, and responsibility for an AI-caused loss splits almost evenly between the tech team (28%), the finance leader (27%) and the employee involved (27%) ([Medius](https://www.medius.com/financial-census/ai-governance/)). That last finding is the most useful for Cosigna: the thresholds exist in people's heads, and nobody owns the loss.

Insurers are adding cost from the other side. ISO generative-AI exclusions have been available since January 2026, about **2,369 GenAI exclusion filings** exist across 49 states, and W.R. Berkley applies an absolute AI exclusion on D&O, E&O and fiduciary lines ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/ai-exclusions-are-spreading--harbor-just-raised-10-million-to-insure-the-gap-594077.aspx)). Specialist cover is forming around controls. Harbor raised a $10M seed to block non-compliant agent decisions before they execute and then insure the residual risk ([same source](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/ai-exclusions-are-spreading--harbor-just-raised-10-million-to-insure-the-gap-594077.aspx)). No source shows a mainstream carrier *requiring* agent spending limits or approvals as a condition of cover. "Controls make you insurable" is a plausible future driver, not a current one.

## Native controls now cover the single-rail case for free

The substitute that matters most moved in 2026, and the earlier picture of Stripe offering only a per-action confirmation link is out of date. Stripe's two-party approval rules can now gate refunds, payment-intent creation, subscription changes and bank-account edits. For **agent-tagged API keys only**, they also gate payouts, outbound payments, outbound transfers and currency conversion. Rules take amount thresholds, actor and rate-limit conditions, can name reviewers by person or role, and prevent self-approval. Default rules are switched on for agent keys, a blocked call returns `approval_required` with the agent's justification attached, and approvals emit webhooks ([Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/account/approvals)). The docs' API version is `2026-09-30.preview`, so parts may still be in preview. Stripe's MCP server will also reject full-access and untagged restricted keys from December 2026 ([Stripe MCP](https://docs.stripe.com/mcp)). For a Stripe-only company that wants agent refunds and payouts approved, Stripe now does most of what Cosigna's first gateway does, for free and by default. The remaining gaps are real but narrower: the agent still holds a Stripe credential, there is one rule per action, the docs show no per-agent cumulative budget, and evidence lives in Stripe's dashboard rather than in an independently verifiable export.

| Substitute | What it covers | What it leaves for Cosigna |
|---|---|---|
| Stripe approval rules + agent keys ([docs](https://docs.stripe.com/account/approvals)) | Amount, actor and rate rules on refunds, payouts and transfers; default on for agent keys; reviewer roles | Other rails; cumulative per-agent budgets; agent still holds a key; evidence outside Stripe |
| Ramp Agent Cards ([docs](https://agents.ramp.com/docs/agent-cards/overview); [blog](https://ramp.com/blog/ai-agent-spending-controls)) | Single-use cards scoped to merchant and amount, expiring within 12 hours; inherit the user's approval chain | Only outbound card spend; no refunds, payouts, bank transfers or ERP |
| Auth0 for AI Agents / Entra Agent ID / Aembit ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/blog/secure-ai-stop-managing-api-keys-by-hand/); [Aembit](https://aembit.io/pricing)) | Agent identity, token vaulting, generic out-of-band consent (CIBA) | No payment semantics: amount, payee, running totals, idempotency |
| ERP/AP approval matrices (NetSuite, Coupa, SAP) | Auditor-accepted approvals for AP inside the ERP | Anything outside the ERP: PSP refunds, direct bank payouts |
| Agent-security platforms (Zenity, Noma) | Runtime tool-call policy, visibility | No finance mandates or exact-transaction approval found |
| Status quo: humans release every payment | Zero agent risk | Loses the automation; 46% have undocumented thresholds ([Medius](https://www.medius.com/financial-census/ai-governance/)) |

Credential brokering is the part of Cosigna under the heaviest commodity pressure. Auth0's Token Vault promises that "agents never need to handle credentials" for a 50% uplift on the base plan ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/blog/secure-ai-stop-managing-api-keys-by-hand/); [Auth0 pricing](https://auth0.com/de/pricing.md)). Microsoft reportedly folds Entra Agent IDs into a **$15 per user per month** Agent 365 licence ([Licensing School](https://www.licensingschool.co.uk/licensing-blog/2026/03/23/agent-365-ga-announcement/)), and Aembit lists **$20 per agent per month** ([Aembit](https://aembit.io/pricing)). Security and identity buyers clearly have budget. The large identity vendors bought their agent-identity pieces in 2025–2026 (CrowdStrike–SGNL at about $740M, Cisco–Astrix at a reported ~$400M, ServiceNow–Veza at about $1.2B) ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/08/crowdstrike-ai-cybersecurity-sgnl-acquisition.html); [guptadeepak.com](https://guptadeepak.com/identity-giants-bought-ai-agent-security/)), so a standalone "agents never hold keys" pitch will be bundled away. Credential brokering should be presented as a property of Cosigna's enforcement, not as what customers pay for.

The buyer is split, and that is a real go-to-market risk. Gartner's first Market Guide for Guardian Agents is written for "security and identity leaders", calls for a "neutral, trusted guardian agent layer" across platforms, and also says deployments are "mainly prototypes or pilots" ([The Hacker News, vendor-contributed](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/5-learnings-from-first-ever-gartner.html)). Deloitte finds that **33% of companies say the CISO owns AI governance and only 19% say the CFO does** ([Deloitte 2Q 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/business-strategy-growth/2q-2026-cfo-signals-survey.html)), yet the felt pain (thresholds, approvals, audit findings) sits with the controller. Finance budgets are small. A 2024 Datarails estimate puts total finance-stack spend at about **$63,600 a year** for companies near $800M in revenue ([Datarails](https://datarails.com/?p=26824)), so a controller-bought tool is a low-five-figure contract unless security co-funds it.

The cross-rail framing holds only above a certain size. **More than 90% of finance teams in a 2026 CFO Connect survey use six or fewer finance tools**, and 65% of firms under 20 employees use one to three ([Spendesk](https://www.spendesk.com/blog/finance-tech-stack-evaluation/)). Merchants typically use two to five payment providers ([Ecommpay](https://ecommpay.com/press-room/merchants-focus-on-functionality-reliability-and-speed-of-payment-providers/)), but that measures pay-ins, not agent-initiated outbound actions. The awkward consequence is that AI-native startups, the earliest and most reachable adopters, tend to run Stripe-centric stacks where Stripe's native approvals are enough. The neutral layer becomes compelling in the mid-market, where an agent touches a PSP, a bank, an AP tool and an ERP, and that is a slower, harder sale.

## Capital floods payment rails while the cross-rail governance seat stays empty

The category is crowded, but the crowd is mostly building a different product. The money has gone to helping agents *pay*: Natural ($30M Series A, no customers disclosed at its beta exit) ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/natural-raises-30m-to-reinvent-payments-for-ai-agents-and-take-on-stripe/)), Catena Labs ($30M Series A, building an AI-native bank) ([FinSMEs](https://www.finsmes.com/2026/05/catena-labs-raises-30m-in-series-a-funding.html)), Basis Theory ($33M Series B, credential vault) and Kite ($33M, agent payments chain) ([Rye](https://rye.com/blog/agentic-commerce-startups); [BeInCrypto](https://beincrypto.com/kite-investment-from-coinbase-ventures/)), plus consumer checkout players such as Nekuda, Prava, Crossmint and Skyfire. **Disclosed traction across the category is close to zero**. Skyfire's "thousands of transactions daily" is unverified, and none of the Series A companies publish volumes. The players closest to Cosigna's thesis are small. Europe's Ralio raised a **$2.5M pre-seed** to provide "guardrails needed for autonomous AI systems to transact across B2B financial workflows" ([Finextra](https://www.finextra.com/newsarticle/47569/ralio-raises-25m-for-agentic-payments-platform)). YC's Locus (F25) offers per-transaction permissions and limits with payments "traceable, auditable, and fully reconcilable" ([YC directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/industry/payments)). AIsa raised $6.5M for enterprise controls on payments by "AI employees" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/elainepofeldt/2026/07/03/startup-raises-65-million-by-making-it-easier-for-ai-employees-to-make-payments-online/)). Payman's pivot is instructive. It started as a marketplace where AI pays humans and now sells "policy controls, approvals, and audit trails" for AI banking actions *to banks and credit unions* ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260520362081/en/Payman-AI-Selected-for-ICBA-ThinkTECH-Accelerator)). At least one company found governance easier to sell through a regulated distributor than directly.

Incumbents each cover their own platform and nothing more. Stripe's broader "agent guardrails" (agent identities, scope rules, approval flows) and "Issuing for agents" are still **Preview** ([Stripe Sessions 2026](https://stripe.com/blog/everything-we-announced-at-sessions-2026); [Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/agents)). AWS AgentCore Policy evaluates Cedar rules on tool calls but reportedly requires the approval step to be built separately ([Aperion, secondary](https://aperion.ai/blog/aws-bedrock-agentcore-governance-guide/)). Workday bought C1.ai to add agent permissions to its own system of record ([Agentic Ready](https://www.getreadyforagents.com/news/workday-acquires-c1-ai-agent-identity-governance/)). No incumbent was found offering one mandate across Stripe, bank, cards and ERP, with approval bound to the exact payee and amount, and evidence a third party can verify. The realistic competitive response is integration rather than a single product: Okta or Auth0 for identity, Stripe guardrails for Stripe, the ERP matrix for AP. That stack plausibly covers most of the need for companies standardized on those vendors.

The exit and fundraising signals cut both ways. Agent and non-human identity security is an active M&A market, with deals from about $100M to $1.2B, Palo Alto even buying the AI gateway Portkey for $117M, but **no acquisition of an agent-payments or financial-governance startup** was found ([guptadeepak.com](https://guptadeepak.com/identity-giants-bought-ai-agent-security/)). YC's Summer 2026 Requests for Startups reportedly name "identity, permissions, payments" for agent-native software ([VC Cafe](https://www.vccafe.com/2026/04/28/requests-for-startups-summer-2026-edition/)). YC also funds agent-payment companies in almost every batch (Locus, Sponge, Allowance, Agentcard), so partners will have seen this pitch many times. Cosigna has to explain on its first slide why it is not a checkout wallet and not a Stripe feature.

## Approval and evidence sell now; autonomy budgets arrive 2027–2030

Analysts put the control-layer budget later than the hype suggests. Gartner predicts **over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027**, and names inadequate risk controls among the causes ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027)). It forecasts that "guardian agents" will take **10–15% of the agentic AI market by 2030** ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-11-gartner-predicts-that-guardian-agents-will-capture-10-15-percent-of-the-agentic-ai-market-by-2030)). Agent-mediated commerce is under 1% of e-commerce today ([Stellagent digest](https://stellagent.ai/insights/ec-ai-news-digest-2026-09-14)). On regulation, the EU AI Act omnibus pushes high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028 ([Sidley](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/06/22/eu-lawmakers-reach-provisional-agreement-to-delay-key-eu-ai-act-obligations/)), and generic payment-execution agents are not obviously high-risk under the Act anyway. The UK and Singapore are consulting but have not yet written rules. Bank of England Deputy Governor Breeden's view that "relying on a human in the loop for all agent actions is unlikely to be realistic" signals the direction: mandate-scoped autonomy with exception approval ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/what-new-rules-agentic-ai-payments-look-like)).

The timing argument for Cosigna therefore splits in two. **Now**: companies piloting agents that prepare payouts and refunds need a way for a human to approve the exact transaction, and a record that satisfies the controller and auditor. That is what lets a pilot get past finance and security review. **In two to three years**: once agents run inside mandates without per-transaction sign-off, cumulative budgets, segregation of duties between agents and portable evidence become a standing budget line, possibly reinforced by insurers and regulators. A startup that sells the first while building toward the second has a coherent story. A startup that sells autonomy controls today is selling to the 9%.

## India builds agent rails while payout agents run on a single OTP

India has the most explicit regulatory signal and the thinnest native controls, though nothing is final. The RBI's FREE-AI framework (August 2025) is non-binding ([RBI](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=61018)). The RBI's authentication directions, in force since 1 April 2026, keep two-factor authentication as the baseline ([RBI](https://website.rbi.org.in/web/rbi/-/press-releases/rbi-issues-directions-on-framework-on-authentication-mechanisms-for-digital-payment-transactions)). Reuters, citing unnamed sources, reports that NPCI is building a "Unified Agent Protocol" so agents can pay on UPI within preset limits without approving each transaction, plus an agent registry starting with small, frequent consumer payments. NPCI has published **no specification, launch date or liability framework** ([The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/payments/news/india-prepares-agentic-payments-rollout-on-upi-network); [TNGlobal](https://technode.global/2026/09/12/india-npci-ai-agent-registry-upi-payments/)). The most important statement is the NPCI chairman's at GFF 2026: **"AI may recommend, but authentication and final settlement must follow deterministic auditable rules"**, and decision and execution "must remain separate" ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-npci-ai-agents-upi-payments/)). That is close to a description of Cosigna's architecture. Dvara Research notes that liability for an agent acting within a valid mandate but against the user's interest is unresolved, and that UPI's dispute framework has no category for it ([same source](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-npci-ai-agents-upi-payments/)).

On the business side, the rails are shipping agents with minimal governance. **RazorpayX's Payout Agent, in beta since June 2026, prepares payouts from a natural-language request and routes them to an OTP approval page**. The announcement mentions no maker-checker, no per-agent limits and only "a few businesses" using it ([Razorpay newsroom](https://razorpay.com/newsroom/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/4728)). Razorpay's Agent Studio, built on Anthropic's Claude Agent SDK, is in early access ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/newsroom/razorpay-launches-the-worlds-first-ai-native-agent-studio-for-payments-at-ftx26-powered-by-anthropics-claude/)). Cashfree's MCP server lets AI trigger refunds and payouts ([Cashfree](https://www.cashfree.com/blog/ai-native-payments-mcp-server/)). No Indian startup raise in agent-payment governance was found. The opening is a cross-rail policy and evidence layer for Indian businesses whose agents trigger payouts on RazorpayX or Cashfree, refunds on a gateway and entries in Tally, Zoho or an ERP. It is unvalidated with buyers, and there are three cautions. Indian SMB willingness to pay for software is low. Razorpay or Cashfree can add maker-checker and limits to their own agents in a sprint. And if NPCI builds limits into UPI itself, the consumer-side opportunity disappears. Consumer agentic UPI is realistically a 2027-or-later market. B2B payout agents exist in beta now.

## Verdict: a real but narrow need, strongest at multi-rail payouts

**Is there a need now?** Yes, but not the one in the broadest pitch. There is current demand for **exact-transaction human approval plus audit-grade evidence** for agents that prepare money movements. That is the gate that moves a finance-agent pilot through controller, security and auditor review. There is no proven current demand for autonomy controls at scale, because almost nobody grants autonomy at scale. There is little standalone willingness to pay for limits or credential brokering on a single rail, because Stripe, Ramp and the identity vendors now give those away.

**Who has it most acutely?** The sharpest pain sits with companies that meet all three of these conditions: they **build their own agents** (Team8 reports two-thirds of enterprises build agents in-house ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/5-learnings-from-first-ever-gartner.html))), those agents **trigger outbound money on two or more rails** (payouts, refunds, vendor payments plus an ERP posting), and they face **an external reviewer**: an auditor, a bank partner, an enterprise customer's security questionnaire, or a regulator. Concretely, this means marketplaces and gig platforms paying out to sellers or workers, lending and fintech operations teams, mid-market finance teams piloting AP or refund agents outside their ERP, and vendors that sell money-moving agents to enterprises and must prove control to close deals. Stripe-only startups and companies that keep agents inside Ramp or their ERP have the need least.

**Strongest wedge for a two-person YC team.** The narrowest defensible starting point is **an approval-and-evidence gateway for payouts initiated by in-house agents, spanning a bank-payout rail plus Stripe**, and sold on audit readiness rather than autonomy. Payouts are the right lead action. They are irreversible or weakly reversible, so a mistake is costly. The bank-payout APIs, where the RazorpayX Payout Agent relies on a single OTP, have the weakest native agent controls. And unlike refunds, they are not covered by every support platform's own human-in-the-loop step. The evidence bundle, which records the mandate, the exact approved payee and amount, the executed call and the provider outcome, verifiable without trusting Cosigna, is the differentiator that Stripe's dashboard logs and Ramp's approval chain do not provide. India is a reasonable first market for speed of access and regulatory alignment. A US design partner is still needed to show YC that the pain exists where budgets are larger. A second wedge worth testing in interviews is **selling to agent vendors**, the AP-agent, support-agent and payout-agent startups that must pass enterprise security review. Payman's pivot suggests a distributor can carry governance faster than direct sales. Treat that as a hypothesis.

**Biggest reasons it might fail.** First, **platform absorption**: Stripe's approval rules are already on by default for agent keys, Stripe's guardrails and Issuing for agents are in preview, and Razorpay and Cashfree can ship maker-checker on their own payout agents. Each rail closing its own gap leaves only the cross-rail seam, which may be too small at the size of company that adopts first. Second, **demand stuck at human-in-the-loop**: if 90% of finance teams keep a human releasing every payment, existing ERP and bank maker-checker workflows already do that, and Cosigna's marginal value is the evidence format alone. Third, **a split buyer with small budgets**: the CISO owns the budget and the controller owns the pain, and finance stacks run to tens of thousands of dollars a year. Fourth, **no forcing event**: with no binding rule, no mainstream loss and no insurer requirement yet, a buyer can postpone indefinitely, and Gartner expects 40% of agent projects to be cancelled. Fifth, **integration drag**: every rail has different idempotency, webhook and reversal behaviour, and two people can maintain only a few rails well. Sixth, **crowding and pattern-matching**: YC sees agent-payments pitches every batch, and Locus and Ralio occupy adjacent positions.

## Fifteen interviews should test pull, multi-rail scope and who pays

The 15 interviews should be weighted toward the acute segment rather than spread evenly. A useful mix is roughly five companies building in-house agents that touch payouts or refunds, four controllers or finance-ops leads at mid-market companies piloting AP or refund agents, three vendors selling money-moving agents to enterprises, two Indian businesses on RazorpayX or Cashfree payouts, and one auditor or internal-audit lead to validate the evidence format. Every interview should be about **past behaviour**, not opinions about the future. The table below lists the questions that separate real demand from polite interest.

| Question to ask | Green flag | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| "Walk me through the last time an agent (or script) prepared a payment, payout or refund. Who released it, and where?" | A named agent in production or pilot that touches money; release happens in a different system from preparation | "We're exploring"; agents only summarize or draft |
| "Which systems does that agent touch to complete one task?" | Two or more money rails plus a ledger or ERP (e.g. RazorpayX + Stripe + Tally/NetSuite) | Stripe only, or everything inside Ramp/the ERP |
| "What blocked the last agent pilot from going wider?" | Security review, the controller, an auditor or a bank partner asked for limits, approvals or logs | Accuracy or model quality; no governance objection raised |
| "Have you turned on Stripe approval rules / Ramp agent cards / your ERP approval matrix for agents? What's missing?" | Tried them and names a specific gap: cross-rail budget, evidence export, non-Stripe rail | Unaware of them, or "that covers it" |
| "Has an auditor, bank or enterprise customer asked how you control or evidence agent actions?" | A specific request, questionnaire item or audit finding they can show you | No external party has asked |
| "Where are your approval thresholds for agent actions written down today?" | Spreadsheet, Slack, someone's head; they admit it would not survive an audit | Formal DoA already enforced in-system |
| "Has an agent, bot or automation ever paid, refunded or posted something wrong? What did it cost?" | A concrete incident or near miss, with an amount | None, and no fear of one |
| "Who would sign for this, and from which budget?" | A named owner with budget (CISO, controller or head of platform) who agrees to a paid pilot | "Need to figure out who owns this"; budget passes between teams |
| "Would you route a live payout through our gateway in a sandbox this month?" | Yes, with a date and a test account | "Send us a deck"; wants to wait for GA |
| (Agent vendors) "How do you answer customers' questions about what your agent is allowed to pay?" | Lost or delayed deals over it; would embed a third-party control layer | Customers don't ask, or they've built it in-house already |

A strong signal is **three or more of the 15 agreeing to a paid or committed pilot within 30 days, with at least two of them on more than one rail**. A clear kill signal is a majority who say Stripe's or their ERP's native approvals already cover them, or who cannot name anyone outside the company asking for the evidence.

## Conclusion

The market evidence changes what Cosigna is. It is not primarily a safety system for autonomous agents, because those barely exist in finance. It is an **admission ticket**: the control and evidence that lets an agent touching money get past the controller, the security team and the auditor at all. That reframing matters for the YC application. "Agents will move trillions" is a 2028 slide that every agent-payments company already uses. The defensible line is that today's blocker to finance agents is that no one can prove what an agent was allowed to do across the systems it touched, and that Cosigna sells that proof.

The deeper strategic insight is that the incumbents' 2026 moves shrink Cosigna's *surface* while validating its *design*. Stripe defaulting approvals on for agent keys, NPCI's chairman insisting on "deterministic auditable rules", ISACA treating missing tamper-evident logs as a finding and insurers pricing AI exposure all confirm that propose-then-approve with verifiable evidence is the accepted pattern. Each of those actors, however, implements it inside its own walls. Cosigna therefore survives only by staying neutral across rails and portable in its evidence. If the first interviews show that customers' agents live on a single rail, the founders should treat that as a reason to pivot toward agent vendors or regulated distributors rather than to compete with Stripe on Stripe's own ground.
